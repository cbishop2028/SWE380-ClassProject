 ## Background
The project our group is involved in is examining how specifications with explicit acceptance criteria, structured scenarios, or file references predict fewer downstream fixes. We first used the “SpecMine Official — 500-Repository Sample Dataset” to examine only a small part of the dataset in order to view the amount of commits that were made to specific specs. In doing so, we created Python files combined with duckdb to build a local database on the user’s device that would then be compiled into a table of averages and a bar chart for the user to view. 
The table that was created from this database specifically listed the number of specs with an acceptance criteria, average word count, average later commits, average fix-like commits, and the amount of fix-like later commits. In doing so, the user was then able to accurately view the average amount of commits that were made, and the data from that stated that the higher the word count the more “fix” commits were made.

## Method
Originally in Sprint 1, our dataset had only taken data from the 500-repository sample dataset, which had an effect of possibly skewing our data. In this Sprint, we are going to instead take the whole dataset and filter down the amount of specs with and without acceptance criteria in specifically a thirty-day window. Some forms of commits that are not fix-like, such as ones done by bots or merges, would have the possibility to skew our data, which means that a sufficient filter would have to be put in place to remove such data from our averages. In addition, we would create a rubric in order to score the specs based on their acceptance criteria, structured scenarios, and file references, therefore each spec would be assigned a maximum amount of points (0.4, 0.3, and 0.3) with the absolute maximum score being 1.0.

## Preliminary results
After our changes to the code and averaging the new size of the sample dataset, we have learned the specs that lacked acceptance criteria (439,894) continued to be drastically larger than the amount that did have acceptance criteria (28,413). 
For our table displaying the fix-like commits in the thirty-day window, we noticed that the average word count for the specs with acceptance criteria (969) wasn’t much larger than the specs without (802). This paralleled what we had seen with the sample dataset but the word count is relatively similar with the full dataset. The average of later commits was also similar, with no acceptance criteria having 0.43 and the specs with acceptance criteria having 0.42. The average of fix-like commits was also very close, with no acceptance criteria having slightly less (0.09) than the specs with criteria (0.104). The portion of criteria with any form of fix-like commits demonstrated that of the 407,547 specs without acceptance criteria within the thirty-day window was 0.052, while the specs with acceptance criteria within the thirty-day window was 0.064. Finally, the fix rate for the two categories was 0.209 for no acceptance criteria and 0.25 for specs with acceptance criteria.
In the Commit Filtering table, we got a result of 304,308 had later commits done in total, with 74,159 of those being outside the thirty-day window. The table also stated that 1,152 merge-commits were removed from the data and 1,937 bot-commits were also removed. The final amount of commits that were within the window and not commits from either merges or bots was 227,060.
